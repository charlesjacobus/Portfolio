    <div class="title">Monochrome</div>
    <div class="callout">
        <div class="calloutLine"><span class="calloutSpeaker">Don Wall:</span> Do ideas have tactility?</div>
        <div class="calloutLine"><span class="calloutSpeaker">John Hejduk:</span> No. Not much for me. Not any more. Ideas did at one time, but now there are so few. Like the Cubists, yes. Mondrian.</div>
        <div class="calloutSource">From John Hejduk's <i>Mask of Medusa</i></div>
    </div>
    <div class="paragraph">Art history and ekphrasis aside, over the years I've returned to just a few literary works concerning art, feeling they summarize well enough whatever thoughts I may share on the matter: <a href="https://www.artforum.com/features/last-exit-painting-208523/" target="_blank">Last Exit: Painting</a> by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Lawson_(artist)" target="_blank">Thomas Lawson</a>, <a href="https://newleftreview.org/issues/i42/articles/john-berger-the-moment-of-cubism" target="_blank">The Moment of Cubism</a> by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Berger" target="_blank">John Berger</a>, and <a href="https://www.dekooning.org/documentation/words/what-abstract-art-means-to-me" target="_blank">What Abstract Art Means to Me</a> by <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willem_de_Kooning" target="_blank">Willem de Kooning</a>, for example. Most of what I feel about art I've picked up along the way, in other sources also not typically prescriptive. To borrow from de Kooning, "I frankly do not understand the question".</div>
    <div class="paragraph">Exactly how I arrived at my first monochrome painting, I cannot say now for certain. What I can say for certain is that with a particular cadmium red square painting I discovered three things at once that set my course, for better or worse. First, after much rework and self-resistance I stumbled upon a solution to a question about "resolving" the spatial plane that I hadn't known to ask, by covering the canvas in a single hue. Then, like magic, I transformed this new-found object by simply pivoting it 45°, to form a "lozenge". And finally, with a few considerate steps back and forward again (as one should do when really looking at a painting) I came to see a sort of drawing in the otherwise monochrome surface by the simple application of paint this way or that, via light refraction. Now, to borrow from <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hejduk" target="_blank">John Hejduk</a>: "color on" versus "color through".</div>
    <div class="paragraph">It was very exciting to discover these sources as I developed my nagging little idea about initially the sense of touch and later a more subtle and complex understanding of the relationship between our tactile senses and visual perception and conception. Along the way I made paintings like this one, which incorporate objects on (under?) the surface. I felt at the time this was a clever approach to capturing the enormity of those Texas landscapes that, through an aggressive suburban development, I saw disappearing around me.</div>
    <img class="imageNarrowWidth" src="/assets/images/monochrome/Monochrome.01.jpg" title="Texas Landscape" />
    <div class="label">1990 : oil on newspaper and plastic farm animals, mounted on board : 22 x 17.5 inches</div>
    <hr />
    <div class="paragraph">Naively but happily, by self-imposing a literal focus on touch and engagement with the entire body, the work demanded a physical solution &mdash; sculptural and architectural form as a framework against which to explore various ideas. What I dreamed up were inventions: endless cusped-wave bas-reliefs, "felt" (as in, to feel) paintings, the <b>Nose Furniture</b>, and the <b>Handrail Paintings</b>. (Notably, each of these a series, which is a practice I continue today.)</div>
    <div class="paragraph">The painting shown here is the sole surviving example, aside from some smaller studies. The idea was simple and divine. The paintings were a manifestation of theory and practice (Foundation) over which I designed in great detail monumental ziggurats (Structure), the imagined experience of ascending and descending was felt by looking at the paintings (Handrail).</div>
    <img class="imageNarrowWidth" src="/assets/images/monochrome/Monochrome.06.jpg" title="Handrail Painting" />
    <div class="label">1995 : oil on canvas : 62 x 62 inches</div>
    <hr />
    <div class="paragraph">The <b>Handrail paintings</b> are a lifetime away in conception and experience from the kinds of things that would come next, despite a mere decade passing and the superficial similarities in this next example with its familiar ochre coloring.</div>
    <div class="paragraph">The <b>Django Parking</b> series is primarily concerned with line and drawing and a certain restrictive kind of pattern and decoration. Clearly, "color on" and consequentially a visual aesthetic. Although perhaps not evident by this example, the drawing motif in this series is a weave - so a similar over/under structure but entirely on the surface, or at least highly compressed. (This painting is a study of sorts - a fragment - where a larger work would have consisted of many such sections.)</div>
    <img class="imageNarrowWidth" src="/assets/images/monochrome/Monochrome.10.jpg" title="Django Parking" />
    <div class="label">2006 : oil on sized linen : 11 x 11 inches</div>
    <div class="paragraph">Key to the idea is the absence of applied color, in the natural weave of the linen support. In this way, I allowed myself the liberty of thinking of the woven bands as lines and therefore these paintings as drawings. The linen material color surely challenges the assertion that the painting is monochrome. But so does it challenge exactly what one should consider the painting.</div>
    <hr />
    <div class="paragraph">Monochrome definitionally and conventionally means of a single color. And while few would argue that this extends to tints and shades and tones, there is a case to be made that it could describe an overall-ness in effect. In other words, the immediate aesthetic impact may also be monochromatically felt without the work being strictly so.</div>
    <div class="paragraph">This last example, from the series <b>A Death in the Family</b>, borrows the band motif ("felt" paintings, <b>Django Parking</b>) but now as a compositional device to return to field. The bands provide a regular metre, while the nuance in painterly handling encourages an off-step and generally slow tempo. The lavender and periwinkle and complementary grays meet bands of light, achieved in this fragment both through over-painting (wet on wet) and by a stripping away, through dry brush lifting color to allow wisps of earlier underpainting to come through. Time measured; time experienced.</div>
    <img class="imageReallyNarrowWidth" src="/assets/images/monochrome/Monochrome.15.jpg" title="A Death in the Family (detail)" />
    <div class="label">2008 : oil on sized canvas : 86 x 5 inches</div>
    <div class="paragraph">This ongoing (lifetime) series of paintings provides a kind of closure over the <b>Handrail Paintings</b>, by continuing a long-standing interest in tactility while exploring a much more nuanced handling of monochromatic form. <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_McLuhan" target="_blank">McLuhan</a>'s take, explored in several books but most affectingly for me in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Gutenberg_Galaxy" target="_blank">The Gutenberg Galaxy</a>, and which took years for me to appreciate, is that tactility is not simply a sensory property of touch, but an interplay among the senses. We quite literally could not see or act in the world without our tactile senses. Where there was for my younger self the discovery of the field condition or the elaborated framework for a singularly bizarre architectural program by way of imagined experience, there is now memory.</div>
